Introduction

Welcome to the QuickEasy Software accounting tutorials.

This section of our website is aimed at non-accountants. We've made these accounting tutorials for people with very little background in accounting theory but who want to learn — as quickly as possible. Whether you're a business owner, general administrative staff, or anyone else who wants to understand accounting, if you want a quick and easy guide, you're in the right place.

We will start with a bird's-eye view first, looking at details as we progress to the end of this accounting tutorial. In the first few chapters we'll be looking at financial statements, and thereafter more closely at practical aspects toward the end. We are using the word "accounting" in the sense that most non-accountants understand it — the task of collecting and sorting an organisation's financial information. At all times we will keep to plain and simple English.

VAT — The What and How

Value Added Tax is often a somewhat mystical concept. We offer a simple explanation of how it works.

Accounting takes everyday business transactions, records them and presents them in a standard form. Two parts of this sentence need some explanation.

"business transactions": a woman walks into a shoe shop, picks up a pair of shoes she likes, walks to the counter and hands over some money. The cashier takes the money, puts the shoes in a bag and gives her a receipt. Both smile and the happy shopper walks out the door. We've all done something like this before — but what we might not realise is that we have just been involved in a business transaction.

"records them": whether it is a handwritten invoice, cash register or fancy computer, these are all ways in which businesses record transactions. In the transaction above, the cashier, when he gives the woman her receipt, has added this event into the shop's accounting system.

Now as you can imagine, transactions take place every day — a few times a day for six or seven days a week, fifty-two weeks a year. The shop owner also pays rent to the landlord, pays staff, buys shoes from the distributor, and so on. At the end of the month or year, he has to give an account of his activities for that period. Below is an example of what this account of activities may look like — this is an income statement.

You may have heard it called something else, like "profit and loss statement" or "statement of comprehensive income". In essence, this statement given by the owners and/or managers of the business is meant to give us an idea of how their business has performed — for example, how many other expenses the shoe shop incurred in making its sales, and how much profit the business made after expenses, by looking at cost of sales. Other standard forms include the statement of changes in equity, balance sheets, and more summarised forms of income statements.

So who uses this kind of information? There are two categories of parties interested: those outside the business who have an interest in how it performs, and those inside the business who need to manage it.

  • Outside the business: SARS, who wants to know how much profit the business has made so it can collect its share of earnings; banks, who want to make sure the business can repay any loan; shareholders, customers, suppliers and staff.
  • Inside the business: owners and managers who track past sales, cost of sales and other expenses, compared to past performance or budgeted/targeted results.

It's interesting to note that all of these formal statements look exactly the same — if you took the name of the business off the page, you wouldn't know what the business does just by looking at the standard report. Information for internal management, on the other hand, tends to be informal and varied: sales reports by customer, product or region, production reports and costing reports. Managers and owners can put the information in pretty much any form that helps them run their business better.

GAAP Compliant

The next portion of our accounting tutorials covers GAAP (pronounced "GAP"), which stands for Generally Accepted Accounting Principles — a group of commonly used accounting standards and principles used for financial reporting. These work to improve transparency and consistency in financial statements from one organisation to the next, and are specific to the country and industry.

Is QuickEasy BOS GAAP compliant?

Yes — BOS is GAAP compliant, in that it provides financial statements and accounting reports based on generally accepted accounting principles. When a company issues and distributes its financial statements to the public, showing revenue recognition, balance sheet item classification and outstanding share measurements, GAAP must be followed. If a company uses GAAP and non-GAAP standards, it needs to identify the non-GAAP measures in financial statements and other public disclosures, such as press releases.

What are the GAAP principles?

The accounting for a business is kept separate from the personal financials of its owner or any other business — none of the owner's personal assets are listed on the business balance sheet, and personal expenses aren't listed against the business's expenses.

Accounting assumes a business will continue to operate, and asset values reflect that — if it's known the business is closing, asset values decline because they must be sold under unfavourable circumstances, and such values often can't be determined until they're actually sold.

Accountants' evaluations and estimates should be fair and reasonable, neither overstating nor understating the business's affairs. Objective records mean that different people looking at the same evidence arrive at the same values — the source document for a transaction is usually the best objective evidence, showing the amount agreed by the buyer and seller.

Accounting takes place over specific, equal-length fiscal periods, used to measure a business's financial progress. Revenue is recognised at the time a transaction is completed — for a cash transaction, income is recorded when the sale is completed and cash received. Getting this right matters: otherwise the company's earnings statements will be incorrect and readers of the financial statement misinformed.

Each expense item (cost of sales) related to income generated must be recorded in the same accounting period as the revenue it helped earn, so the results of operations are measured fairly. The value recorded for an asset isn't changed later just because market value changes — changing it would require an entirely new transaction based on new objective evidence; if no objective evidence is available, the transaction is recorded at fair market value determined independently.

Accountants must be consistent when applying methods and procedures from period to period. Any change must be clearly explained on the financial statements — this prevents people from changing methods purely to manipulate figures. Generally accepted principles are followed except where doing so would be expensive or difficult and ignoring them makes no real difference, provided net income isn't significantly affected and readers can still judge the financial statements fairly. Finally, any information affecting full understanding of a company's financial statements must be included, whether directly or as accompanying notes.

The Accounting Equation

The Accounting Equation: Assets (A) = Equity (E) + Liabilities (L)

If you've spent any amount of time in an accounting course or classroom — or now with our accounting tutorials — this formula should not be new to you. You may have seen a different variant since, being an equation, it could be stated differently (e.g. Equity = Assets − Liabilities).

Previously we've looked at the definitions of assets, liabilities and equity — this helped us understand what they are. The accounting equation helps us understand how they relate to each other: the sum of all the business's assets will always equal the total equity and liabilities. Put another way: the value of all the cash, machinery, vehicles and other assets will always equal the sum of the profits and monies contributed by the owners (equity) and the monies borrowed from a third party (liabilities).

Here's how this makes sense: if a business buys a piece of equipment, it can either use its own resources (cash or other assets), the owner can contribute personal assets, or it can turn to other sources for funding like banks — or a combination of the two.

Note: assets, liabilities and equity are sometimes also referred to as "the balance sheet" — a statement that reflects the assets, liabilities and equity of a person or business on a particular day.

Examples

Let's look at some transactions to help understand this better.

Example 1 — the owner of a company deposits R50,000 into the business bank account. Here's the effect on the balance sheet:

Assets =Equity +Liabilities
+50,000+50,0000

Equity increased by R50,000 because the owner increased his stake in the business — he is risking R50,000 more than before. Since cash is an asset, total assets increase by R50,000 as well.

Example 2 — a loan taken out by the business for a delivery vehicle, R55,000:

Assets =Equity +Liabilities
+55,0000+55,000

Taking out a loan increases the amount owed to people outside the business (liabilities) by R55,000. The company now has a new delivery vehicle, so assets increase by the same amount, keeping the equation in balance.

Example 3 — purchased goods from a supplier on credit, to be sold to customers at a profit. Goods delivered with an invoice for R25,000, payable within 30 days from month end:

Assets =Equity +Liabilities
+25,0000+25,000

We receive goods that go into stock holding until sold — stock (an asset) increases by R25,000. We also owe our supplier money since we're not paying immediately, so liabilities increase by R25,000.

Example 4 — paid R10,000 salaries for the month from the bank account:

Assets =Equity +Liabilities
−10,000−10,0000

Our bank account (an asset) decreases by R10,000. Salaries are expenses, and expenses decrease profits, so equity decreases by R10,000. It helps to remember that the trading account — income (+) and expenses (−) — forms part of equity.

Example 5 — sold goods from stock (value R20,000) for R35,000, customer paid via EFT:

Assets =Equity +Liabilities
+35,000 −20,000+35,000 −20,0000

This is a two-part transaction. First the sale: we have R35,000 more cash in the bank, so assets and equity both increase by R35,000. Then the cost of the sale: R20,000 worth of stock has been sold, decreasing stock on hand and reducing overall profit (equity) via cost of sales.

Example 6 — spent R5,000 in cash on some equipment:

Assets =Equity +Liabilities
+5,000 −5,00000

Here we're only working on one side of the equation — spending money from the bank account decreases assets by R5,000, but buying equipment (also an asset) increases assets by R5,000, netting to no change overall.

A thorough grasp of the accounting equation will help you deal with transactions later on in our next accounting tutorials — spend some time here before moving on.

Assets, Liabilities and Equity

Most people have heard of assets, liabilities and equity, especially when dealing with accounting at school, in a course, or when doing practical bookkeeping. It's important to really understand these concepts since they're at the foundation of much of what we do in accounting.

Assets

For accounting purposes, here's a good working definition of what assets are:

  • Assets belong to the business
  • Assets have a re-sale value or a cash value, and
  • Assets are usually used for running the business and generating income.

Examples include cars, equipment or machines, land and buildings, cash in the bank, money owed to the company (debtors), patents or trademarks, and stock. Financial statements sometimes distinguish between Fixed Assets — those a business plans to hold for longer than 12 months, like vehicles, equipment and property — and Current Assets, which are much more short-term: cash, debtors and stock that the company intends to convert to cash within 12 months.

Liabilities

Think of liabilities as money the business owes to someone else — the bank, a supplier, SARS, the landlord, etc. (This specifically excludes money owed to the owner.) Examples include loans, hire purchase agreements for machinery or cars, mortgage bonds, trade creditors, tax payable, and VAT. As with assets, some statements distinguish long-term liabilities (repayable more than 12 months out) from current liabilities (repayable within 12 months).

Equity, or Owner's Equity

Owner's equity covers transactions that directly affect the owner: contributions made by owners, loans to and from owners, and all income and expenses. It's useful to think of all income and expenses as part of one big account called the trading account, which falls under equity — more on this when we deal with debits and credits.

The chart of accounts

Below is a standard set of accounts one would often find when using an accounting package — go through them one by one and see if they seem familiar. Each of these can be classified as an Asset, a Liability, or an Equity account.

  • Sales
  • Cost of Sales
  • Accounting Fees
  • Bank Charges
  • Commissions
  • Depreciation
  • Interest Paid
  • Levies
  • Motor Expenses
  • Salaries and Wages
  • Retained Income
  • Members Loans
  • Bank Account
  • Motor Vehicle — at cost
  • Accounts Payable
  • Stock on Hand
  • Accounts Receivable

Accounts are really just a more detailed view of what could be shown simply as Assets, Liabilities and Equity — for example, on a Balance Sheet, "Assets" is split into more detail like Bank, Stock and Accounts Receivable.

Examples of accounting statements

The reports that follow are what accounting is all about. After the hard work of capturing invoices, receipts, payments etc. is done, this is what we're looking for: financial transactions recorded in a form that's easily understood. Here's a list of three popular ones.

Balance Sheet — a snapshot of a business's financial position on a specific day: here's what we own (assets), here's what we owe (liabilities), and here's the difference (equity).

Income Statement (or Profit and Loss Statement) — a report on the trading activities of a business, commonly published for a year of trading, telling us about the income made and the expenses incurred making that income.

Trial Balance — more of a standard report than a financial statement, showing a summary of all accounts and their debit or credit balances — everything, not just trading accounts (Income Statement) or a summary of Assets/Equity/Liabilities (Balance Sheet). Trial balances tend to be used by management and auditors rather than published in financial statements.

Debits and Credits

A brief history

It's tricky to explain debit and credit sides without a little history. Initially, "debit" and "credit" referred to the parts on the page of a book called the Ledger — a book with many pages, each assigned to an account (a page for Rent, one for Bank Charges, etc.), each with a debit side (left) and a credit side (right).

So imagine you've just made an EFT to pay your landlord — you'd find the Rent page and record the payment, then go to the Bank Account page and record the same transaction there. We obviously don't do this by hand anymore, but the accounting software we use today has largely been built around this paradigm. Which raises the question: on which side would you put the transaction in the Rent Account, and on which side in the Bank Account?

A rule to commit to memory

Assets increase on the debit side. Liabilities and Equity increase on the credit side. Memorise this rule — it will help you figure everything else out.

Step by step

Let's walk through a few examples, highlighting the questions you should ask each time — a useful framework for solving debit and credit questions.

Transaction: the owner deposits R50,000 of his own money into the business's bank account — his contribution to the business.

  1. What are the two accounts involved? The Bank Account and the Owner's Contribution Account.
  2. What kind of accounts are these? Bank contains cash, so it's an Asset. Owner's Contribution is an Equity account.
  3. What's the effect on these accounts? Bank increases by R50,000 — since assets increase on the debit side, the Bank Account gets a debit of R50,000. Owner's Contribution (equity) also increases by R50,000.

Transaction: the owner buys equipment for the business worth R5,000, paid via EFT from the business bank account.

  1. Accounts affected? Equipment Account and Bank Account.
  2. What kind of accounts? Both are Asset accounts.
  3. How affected? Bank (an asset) decreases, so it's credited R5,000. Equipment (an asset) increases, so it's debited R5,000. (At any time, the account balance is the difference between its debits and credits — here, a debit balance of R45,000 remains in Bank.)

The most important takeaways: the accounting rules around debits and credits, and the 3-question framework for dealing with transactions. Master these and you're well on your way.

Nominal accounts: income and expenses

Let's take it further with transactions involving income and expenses. Remember the two components: assets increase on the debit side, liabilities and equity increase on the credit side; and the 3-question framework — what accounts are involved, what kind are they, and how are they affected.

Transaction: paid monthly rent to the landlord via EFT.

  1. Accounts involved? Bank Account and Rent Paid Account.
  2. What kind? Bank is an Asset. Rent Paid isn't an asset (it has no resale value) and isn't a liability (it doesn't increase what we owe outsiders) — by elimination, it must be Equity, since equity is a combination of the owner's contributions and the profits of the business's activities.
  3. How affected? Bank decreases, so it's credited.

The wrong approach: "Rent Paid belongs to Equity, and Equity increases on the credit side, therefore Rent Paid is credited" — this skips a step and invites errors. The right approach: Rent Paid falls into the Equity category because it forms part of calculating the business's profit or loss. Think of Profit & Loss as a single ledger account where both the debit and credit sides have their own sub-debit and sub-credit sides — this is why accounts like Sales, Interest Income, Bank Charges and Accounting Fees are called Nominal Accounts (not "real" accounts). Expenses increase on the debit side, income on the credit side.

Example 1 — received interest on bank deposit of R50. Accounts: Bank (Asset) and Interest Received (Income/Equity). Bank increases (Dr R50); Income increases (Cr R50 to Interest Received).

Example 2 — paid R500 cash for materials used in manufacturing. Accounts: Bank (Asset) and Cost of Sales (Equity). Bank decreases (Cr R500); Cost of Sales increases (Dr R500).

Example 3 — sold goods on credit to a customer, R1,200, payable in 30 days. Accounts: Debtors (Asset) and Sales (Income/Equity). Debtors increase (Dr); Income increases (Cr).

Understanding that expense and income accounts are part of Equity is key to handling debit and credit problems — keep going over these examples until the concepts click.

Double-Entry Method

It's important that the Trial Balance report balances after every transaction — meaning the sum of all debits equals the sum of all credits. This is one way we know the accounting has been done correctly. All transactions must affect at least two accounts — this is the golden rule of double-entry accounting that keeps the system balanced. (For now, forget debits and credits — just think about which accounts are affected.)

Example 1 — the owner of a new startup transfers R10 million to the business account for XYZ Printers (Pty) Ltd. Ask: what is different in the accounts of the business after the transaction? The business has R10m more in its Bank Account, and the business now owes the owner R10m more than before. Accounts affected: Bank Account and Shareholders Loan (Equity).

Example 2 — sold printed goods to BBM Phones for R4,500, payable in 30 days. Since we "sold goods", Sales is affected. Since BBM will pay in 30 days, they give us a promise to pay — that's Debtors / Accounts Receivable.

Practice — name the two (or more) accounts involved in each:

  1. Bought stationery from a supplier on account, payable in 30 days. — Stationery and Creditors/Accounts Payable
  2. Paid R5,000 towards a company car loan by debit order. — Bank and Car Loan
  3. Bought food for an office function, paid by EFT. — Entertainment and Bank
  4. Owner bought paper for a job, paid from his personal bank account. — Cost of Sales and Members Loans
  5. Bank debited the account with a R200 monthly service fee. — Bank Charges and Bank
  6. Paid salaries into staff bank accounts via EFT. — Salaries and Bank
  7. Customer paid for a printed job via EFT, with a 10% discount given. — Sales, Bank and Discount Allowed (more than two accounts)
  8. An EFT was received from a customer as payment on their outstanding account. — Bank and Debtors/Accounts Receivable

VAT — What It Is and How It Works

Value Added Tax (VAT) is often a somewhat mystical concept. Here's a simple explanation of what it is, how it works, and how we deal with the accounting side of VAT.

What is VAT?

The simplest way to explain VAT is by example. In a world with no VAT, Mr Farmer sells his produce to Mrs Deli for R20; Mrs Deli turns it into lunch and sells it to Joe Soap for R100.

Inject VAT into this scenario: Mr Farmer, a registered "VAT Vendor" (someone who collects tax on behalf of SARS), sells his produce to Mrs Deli for R22.80 (R20 plus 14% VAT). Mrs Deli, also a VAT vendor, then charges Joe Soap R114 rather than R100.

Why sign up as SARS's little helper? Two reasons: VAT vendors can deduct the VAT they pay on producing their goods or services from what they owe SARS; and it's the law — businesses whose income exceeds a certain threshold (R1 million turnover per annum, at time of writing) must register for VAT.

Mrs Deli pays R22.80 for ingredients (the R2.80 is called Input VAT). She sells a meal to Joe Soap for R114 (the R14 is called Output VAT). At the end of every VAT period (usually bi-monthly), she adds up all her Output VAT and Input VAT and pays the difference to SARS.

How does VAT affect accounting?

Company A sells widgets to Company B for R5,000, paid via EFT — accounts involved: Sales and Bank. Once VAT is added, the customer is charged R5,700 instead — but the company's sales figure stays the same, since only R5,000 belongs to the business; the R700 collected belongs to SARS. A third account, VAT Control, is added: a liability account keeping a running balance of the difference between VAT outputs and inputs. R700 is now owed to SARS.

Add another transaction: the company buys the widgets from Supplier A for R2,850 (R2,500 + R350 VAT), paid via EFT. This affects Cost of Sales, Bank and VAT Control. Bank decreases by R2,850; Cost of Sales is only affected by R2,500, since the R350 VAT portion is deducted from the amount owing to SARS. The VAT Control balance drops from R700 owing to R350 owing.

VAT report

If you're using business operating software, these reports are automatically generated as you capture receipts, payments, customer invoices and supplier invoices — grouping inputs and outputs together and calculating what's due to you or payable to SARS.

Quickfire VAT facts

  • You can only claim VAT on invoices that charge VAT.
  • If you claim VAT on an invoice, keep the document and make sure the supplier has put your VAT number on it.

Conclusion

VAT adds a very slight complexity to the basic accounting you've been learning so far — as in all things, sticking to the basics will help you navigate it easily. Remember, VAT is simply collecting money on behalf of SARS, and in return you get to claim VAT on some of your business expenses. This means keeping track of all sales as well as all expenses you can claim.
